Study guide · Sawhney, Vasavada & Wang (Stanford, NeurIPS 2018 workshop) + reproduction notebook

Latent Feature Extraction for Musical Genres from Raw Audio

The paper asks: can a neural network listen to raw sound (no hand-made features) and learn a short list of numbers that captures a song's genre? The notebook rebuilds the paper exactly, checks whether its numbers hold up, finds a hidden bug, and then builds better models.

ClassicalJazzMetalPop

What problem are they solving?

Style transfer for images is well known: take a photo and repaint it "in the style of Van Gogh". To do that, you need a way to measure style as numbers. For images, people use the middle layers of a trained image classifier (VGG-19) as that measurement.

The authors want the same for music. Music "style" is hard to define, so they use genre as a stand-in: if a network can learn numbers that separate classical from metal, those numbers probably hold something about musical style. These numbers are called an encoding or embedding (a compact summary vector).

Think of an encoding like a song's fingerprint. A 0.9-second clip goes in as 500 numbers, and comes out as 64 numbers (or just 4). If the fingerprint is good, all jazz fingerprints look alike and all metal fingerprints look alike.

The paper in five sentences

  1. Take 400 songs from the GTZAN dataset (100 each of classical, jazz, metal, pop).
  2. Cut each into short clips, shrink each clip to 500 numbers, giving 8,000 examples.
  3. Try three models: a simple classifier, an autoencoder (learns to compress and rebuild), and a hybrid of the two called the Deep Softmax Autoencoder.
  4. The hybrid wins: about 95% train and 65% test accuracy, against 52% / 36% for the simple classifier.
  5. Plots (PCA) of the hybrid's 64-number and 4-number encodings show genres forming separate clouds, so the encodings carry genre information.

The notebook in five sentences

  1. Part 1 rebuilds the paper in PyTorch with every setting traced to the paper or the authors' code.
  2. Train accuracy matches the paper, but test accuracy comes out lower (about 61% averaged over 5 seeds instead of 65%).
  3. It finds a design flaw: the decoder ends with a ReLU, so it "dies" and outputs all zeros. The autoencoder never really learns, and the hybrid model is secretly just a deep classifier.
  4. Part 2 fixes this and improves step by step: fixed autoencoder → more audio → 1-D convolutional network on raw audio (≈87% test) → 2-D convolutional network on mel spectrograms (≈98% test).
  5. It also tests on a fairer song split (test songs are never heard in training), which gives lower but more honest numbers.

Paper §3 · Notebook cells 2–7

How sound becomes numbers

Sound recorded on a computer is a long list of amplitudes: how far the speaker cone is pushed in or out at each instant. GTZAN audio is sampled at 22,050 Hz, which means 22,050 amplitude numbers per second. Values go roughly from −1 to +1, so about half of them are negative. Remember that, it matters later.

1 · datasetGTZAN: 1,000 songs × 30 s, 10 genres
2 · filterKeep 4 genres → 400 songs
3 · loadConvert .au → .wav, read with LibROSA at 22,050 Hz
4 · cut20 clips per song × 20,000 samples (0.907 s each)
5 · shrinkAverage pooling, window 40 → 500 numbers
6 · split8,000 clips → 6,000 train / 1,000 dev / 1,000 test

Why cut songs into clips?

400 songs is a tiny dataset for a neural network. Cutting each 30-second song into 20 clips turns 400 examples into 8,000. This is a form of data augmentation (making more training examples from the data you have).

What is average pooling here?

Each clip has 20,000 numbers. That is a lot of inputs, and a network with 20,000 inputs has many weights and overfits easily. So they take every block of 40 neighbouring numbers and replace it with their average: 20,000 ÷ 40 = 500.

[0.10, 0.12, 0.08, … 40 values …] → mean = 0.11   |   20,000 samples → 500 averages

The paper says this also works as regularisation (it reduces overfitting). The cost is that averaging acts as a low-pass filter: 22,050 ÷ 40 ≈ 551 numbers per second, so only sounds below about 275 Hz survive. Cymbals, vocals' brightness and most of what makes metal sound like metal are thrown away. The notebook points this out in Part 2.

"One second" is not one second. The paper says "one second segments" but also says each segment has 20,000 samples. At 22,050 Hz, 20,000 samples is 0.907 s. The notebook follows the numbers (20,000 → 500, 8,000 examples).

No feature engineering

Most genre-classification work first converts audio into hand-designed features like MFCCs or mel spectrograms. The paper deliberately avoids this. Its goal is to learn style "directly from raw audio". The notebook's Step 4 later shows how much that choice costs.

Paper §4 · Notebook cells 8–16

The paper's three models

All three are fully connected (dense) neural networks: every number in one layer connects to every number in the next. Each takes the 500-number clip as input.

Model 1 · Two-layer neural network (baseline)

Paper §4.1 · 64,644 parameters

The simplest possible classifier, used as a baseline (a reference point to beat).

Shape
500 → 128 (tanh) → 4
What it does
Takes 500 numbers, mixes them into 128 "hidden" numbers, squashes each with tanh, then mixes those into 4 scores, one per genre. Softmax turns the 4 scores into probabilities.
Loss
Cross-entropy (Eq. 1). It punishes the model when it gives low probability to the correct genre.
Training
Adam, learning rate 0.0001, batch 512, 200 epochs.
Result
~52% train, ~36–39% test. Barely better than guessing (25%) on test, and already overfitting.
L_cross-entropy = − Σj=0..3 yj · log(ŷj)

y is the one-hot true label (e.g. jazz = [0,1,0,0]) and ŷ is the predicted probabilities. Only the true genre's term survives, so the loss is just −log(probability given to the right answer). If the model says 0.9 for the right genre, loss = 0.105. If it says 0.1, loss = 2.3.

Model 2 · Vanilla autoencoder

Paper §4.2 · 421,556 parameters

An autoencoder learns to copy its input to its output through a narrow middle layer called the bottleneck. Because the bottleneck is small, the network must learn a compact summary. "Vanilla" just means plain, no extra tricks. It is unsupervised: it never sees the genre labels.

It is like summarising a 500-word essay in 64 words, then asking someone to rewrite the full essay from your summary. A good summary lets them get close.

L = γ · ‖x − g(f(x))‖²₂  +  (1 − γ) · CrossEntropy    with γ = 0.9

The paper writes the second term as "− (1−γ) Σ y log ŷ", which is the same thing because cross-entropy already has a minus sign. γ = 0.9 means 90% weight on rebuilding the audio and 10% on getting the genre right.

Why they think it helps: the classifier sees only 64 numbers instead of 500, so it has fewer weights to overfit with. And the reconstruction task forces the bottleneck to keep general information about the sound, which acts as extra regularisation.

The paper's main claims

  • Classical is easiest (highest F1), which they put down to its distinct style.
  • Main confusions: metal ↔ pop, and classical ↔ jazz (similar instruments).
  • Pop is the most scattered in the plots; the authors say pop has "less of a distinct style" and overlaps the others.
  • The vanilla autoencoder's bottleneck shows no genre separation (expected, it never saw labels). The Deep Softmax AE's bottleneck and logits show separation and "smoothness".
  • 64-dim vs 4-dim: 64 numbers capture finer detail within a genre; 4 numbers give cleaner separation between genres.
  • Train 95% vs test 65% is a big gap, a sign of overfitting that the paper does not dwell on.

Future work they list: use the encodings for real style transfer, add more genres, try MFCC / feature engineering, and try a β-TCVAE to learn disentangled representations (measured with MIG).

Part 1: rebuilding the paper

The notebook's rule: paper first. If the paper does not say, use the authors' GitHub code (commit 00b1802). If the two disagree, the paper wins. It is written in PyTorch (the original was TensorFlow 1.x), runs on CPU with seed 0 and deterministic algorithms so the results repeat exactly.

Where paper and code disagree (paper value used):
  • Loss: code uses mean MSE + 0.1·CE; paper uses 0.9·sum-of-squares + 0.1·CE.
  • Dropout keep probability: code 0.8, paper 0.9.
  • Epochs for the hybrid: code 200, paper's Figure 3 about 500.

Discovery 1: the decoder is dead

The vanilla autoencoder outputs zero for every input. Its loss freezes at 5.6842 from epoch 50 onward and never changes. The notebook checks: 0.0% of the reconstruction values are above zero, and the loss exactly equals Σx² (the energy of the input), which is what you get when the output is all zeros.

Why it happens: the decoder's last activation is a ReLU, which can only output 0 or positive numbers. A waveform is half negative, so the decoder can never copy it well. During training, the last layer's pre-activations got pushed below zero for every input. Once that happens, ReLU outputs 0 and its gradient is also 0, so no learning signal flows back. This is the dying ReLU problem. The encoder behind it stops getting updates too, so the "vanilla AE bottleneck" in Figure 5 is close to a randomly initialised encoder.

Discovery 2: the hybrid is secretly a classifier

The same dead ReLU sits in the Deep Softmax AE's decoder. On the trained model: 0.9 × reconstruction = 5.0996, which equals exactly 0.9 × Σx², and the decoder outputs are 0% positive. A constant term in the loss adds nothing to the gradient. So the reconstruction half does nothing, and the model learns purely as a deep classifier: 500 → 256 → 192 → 128 → 64 → 32 → 16 → 4.

This changes the story. The paper's gain over the baseline comes from a deeper network trained for longer, not from the autoencoder idea. The notebook stresses this is a consequence of the published architecture, not a mistake in the reproduction.

Notebook Part 2 · cells 39–62

Part 2: making it better, one change at a time

Each step adds one change on top of the previous one, so you can see what each change is worth. This is called an ablation-style study.

A fairer way to test: the evaluation protocol

Clip split
The paper's random 6,000 / 1,000 / 1,000 split of clips. Used to compare with the paper.
Song split
Whole songs go to one side only: 80 / 10 / 10 songs per genre. Exact duplicate recordings (18 songs share identical audio with another) are kept together.
5 seeds
Every final model runs with seeds 0–4 on both splits; results are mean ± standard deviation.
Metrics
Clip accuracy, test macro-F1, and song accuracy (average the softmax over all of a song's test clips, then pick the top genre).
No test peeking
Every choice (γ, pooling, extra audio) is picked by dev accuracy on clip split, seed 0. Test is never used to choose.
Why the clip split is leaky. With random clips, test clips come from songs the model also trained on: in seed 0, the test set's clips come from 362 songs that also have clips in training. The model can partly recognise the song (same singer, same recording) instead of the genre. That inflates accuracy. The song split removes this leak, which is why its numbers are lower.

Fixed Deep Softmax AE (Step 1–2)

Notebook
Architecture
Identical to the paper's, except the decoder's final layer has no activation (linear), so it can produce negative amplitudes.
Loss
γ · mean((x − x̂)²) + (1 − γ) · CE. Reconstruction is averaged over input values so γ means the same thing at any input size. γ = 0 means pure classifier.
Input scaling
Standardised with the training set's mean and standard deviation: (x − μ) / σ
Training
Adam lr 0.0001, batch 512, up to 500 epochs, early stopping after 50 epochs with no dev improvement, keep best epoch
Lesson
Fixing the bugs alone barely helps (61.0 → 61.5%). The dense design on heavily pooled audio is the real limit.

Convolutional Deep Softmax AE (Step 3, raw waveform)

Notebook

A convolution slides a small filter along the signal and looks for a pattern (a drum hit, a note onset) wherever it occurs. A dense layer must learn each position separately. Convolution shares the same weights at every position, so it needs far fewer weights and recognises patterns anywhere in the clip.

Input
Waveform average-pooled by 4 → 5,000 numbers per clip (≈5,512 Hz)
Encoder
4 blocks of Conv1d(kernel 9, stride 4) → BatchNorm → ReLU, channels 1 → 16 → 32 → 64 → 128
Global pooling
Average and max over time, joined → 256 numbers → dropout → Linear 256 → 64 + ReLU = the 64-dim encoding
Decoder
Linear back to a short feature map, then 4 transposed convolutions (kernel 8, stride 4) that rebuild the waveform
Classifier head
The paper's 64 → 32 → 16 → 4
Training
Adam lr 0.001, batch 64, up to 40 epochs, patience 8
Sweeps
Pool 4 beat pool 8. γ = 0 won, meaning the decoder is switched off and this is a pure CNN classifier.
Result
87.2% clip test, 83.2% song-split test, 91% song accuracy

Mel-spectrogram Deep Softmax AE (Step 4, best model)

Notebook · best

A spectrogram shows which frequencies are loud at each moment, like a heat-map of sound: time across, pitch up, brightness = loudness. A mel spectrogram spaces the frequency rows the way human hearing does (fine detail in low pitches, coarser in high). Taking the log (decibels) matches how we perceive loudness.

Input
Each 20,000-sample clip → LibROSA mel spectrogram (64 mel bands, FFT size 1,024, hop 512) → power_to_db → a 64 × 40 image
Scaling
Standardised separately for each mel band
Encoder
4 blocks of Conv2d(3×3) → BatchNorm → ReLU → MaxPool(2×2), channels 16 → 32 → 64 → 128, then global avg + max pool → 256 → Linear → 64
Decoder
Transposed 2-D convolutions (kernel 4, stride 2) that rebuild the spectrogram
Head / training
Same as Step 3; γ = 0.1 won the sweep
Result
97.7% clip test, 94.5% song-split test. Per-genre F1 on the test set: 0.97 classical, 0.96 jazz, 0.99 metal, 0.99 pop.

Why it works so much better: the spectrogram keeps the full frequency range (no 275 Hz cut-off), and it lays out pitch and timbre directly, which is exactly what separates genres. A 2-D CNN treats it like an image and finds local time-frequency patterns.

Five takeaways

  1. Train-test gap: the paper's model memorises (95% train) but generalises poorly (≈60% test).
  2. Architecture beats tweaks: bug fixes and more data gave +3 points; switching to convolutions gave +23.
  3. Input representation matters most: mel spectrograms add another +10 points.
  4. The autoencoder part barely matters: the winning γ values were 0 or 0.1, so the models are mostly classifiers.
  5. Song accuracy > clip accuracy: averaging many clips from one song cancels out individual mistakes.

One-minute recap

Paper: learn genre encodings from raw audio for future music style transfer. 4 GTZAN genres, 8,000 clips of 500 averaged samples. Three models: two-layer NN (36% test), vanilla autoencoder (no labels, no genre separation), Deep Softmax Autoencoder (autoencoder + classifier on the 64-dim bottleneck, loss 0.9·reconstruction + 0.1·CE, 65% test). Classical easiest, pop most scattered, metal↔pop and classical↔jazz confused.

Reproduction: train accuracy matches; test is lower (61% over 5 seeds). The decoder's final ReLU dies, outputs all zeros, so reconstruction is a constant and the "hybrid" is really just a deep classifier.

Improvements: fix decoder + standardise + early stopping (61.5%) → more clips (64.3%) → 1-D CNN on raw audio (87.2%) → 2-D CNN on log-mel spectrograms (97.7%). The fairer song split gives lower but honest numbers (94.5% for the best model).
